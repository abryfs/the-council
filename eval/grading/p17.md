# p17

**Prompt the founder typed:**
> honestly I think we've built too much. There are 9 features in the app and I'd guess 3 get used. I don't know how to decide what to cut without feeling like I'm throwing away work. Walk me through it.

_Context: Six months in, feature list keeps growing and shipping keeps slipping_

For EACH set below, judge EACH pattern: is it genuinely relevant to this specific prompt — would surfacing it change or improve how an agent handles this request? Be strict. A pattern that is merely topically adjacent is NOT relevant.


## Set A

- **Sunk Identity** — A decision defended by prior investment or by "this is what we are" cannot be evaluated, because both premises are facts about the past rather than about the option. The incumbent option gets an unearned advantage precisely in proportion to how much was spent establishing it. TRIGGER: A decision or spec is defended by how much has already been built, how long it took, or by identity language ("we're a X company", "this is our thesis"), rather than by the option's forward value.
- **Second-System Effect** — The v2 becomes the dumping ground for every idea deferred from v1, and because none of them has shipped, none can be cut on evidence. Scope grows faster than the team can integrate, so the system never reaches a state where any single feature is finishable. TRIGGER: A spec or plan for a v2/next-gen version that lists features deferred from v1, or bundles a new data model + new UI + new extensibility layer in one release.
- **Skipping The Unscalable** — Automating the first-user experience before doing it by hand encodes your guesses about what users need into software, and the automation then hides the disconfirming evidence. Manual onboarding is not inefficiency; it is the only instrument fine-grained enough to find the problem that the metrics summarize away. TRIGGER: A plan to build self-serve signup, an onboarding wizard, an email drip, an in-app tour, or a help center before roughly ten users have been onboarded by a founder in person.
- **Preserving The Shape** — When a change is bolted onto an existing structure specifically to avoid disturbing it — because touching it would trigger a migration, recertification, or retraining cost — the new behavior inherits constraints it was never designed for, and the reason to avoid them becomes a reason not to fix them. TRIGGER: A change explicitly scoped to avoid touching an existing schema, interface, migration, or retraining/re-certification cost — phrases like "without changing the existing", "to avoid a migration", "keep it backward compatible" driving the design rather than following it.
- **Built On Rented Land** — A distribution channel owned by another company is revocable on their schedule, and they revoke it exactly when you become large enough to matter or when they want the market themselves. The dependency is invisible during the growth phase because the channel is working, which is the same evidence that makes you a threat. TRIGGER: A growth mechanism that depends on another company's API, social graph, app-store placement, feed distribution, marketplace listing, extension store, or OAuth integration as the primary source of new users.
- **Refusing The Amputation** — Deferring a feature to 'phase 2' keeps it in the architecture — its data model, its surface area, its coupling — so the v1 carries the full complexity cost while delivering none of the value. Deletion is the only scope cut that actually reduces work; deferral is bookkeeping. TRIGGER: A v1 spec preserves every feature from a prototype or predecessor, and the scope-cut discussion produces deferrals ('phase 2', 'later slice', 'roadmap') rather than removals from the model.
- **Free Tier With No Wall** — A free plan converts only where its limit sits exactly at the point a serious user becomes dependent; limits on axes casual users never hit (storage, history, time) give away the whole product and leave the paid tier selling nothing. Fixing this later means taking something back from existing users, which is a far more expensive act than setting the wall correctly at launch. TRIGGER: A spec adding a free plan, free tier, or open beta whose limits are storage, seats-of-one, retention window, or time, rather than the specific capability a committed user cannot work without; also any "free forever" framing.
- **Adding People To A Late Thing** — New people on a late project consume the time of the people who know the system, and communication paths grow quadratically with headcount, so the near-term effect on a component with tight internal coupling is negative. Partitionable work is the exception, and the work that is late is rarely the partitionable kind. TRIGGER: A slipping deliverable is answered by assigning more engineers, more parallel agents, or more concurrent sessions to the same component or file set, rather than by cutting scope.
- **Control Plane Inside The Blast Radius** — When the tooling that observes, deploys, authenticates, or rolls back depends on the system it manages, the first failure removes the means of recovery. Mean time to repair is then set by whatever out-of-band path was never built. TRIGGER: A design where monitoring, deploy, auth, feature flags, or the rollback path runs on, resolves through, or authenticates against the same system it controls.
- **One-Way Door, Casual** — Decisions get processed at the weight of their immediate cost, not their reversibility, so a cheap-to-decline strategic option receives less scrutiny than an expensive-to-build feature. The irreversibility only becomes visible after the window that would have let you act on it has closed. TRIGGER: A decision that forecloses a future option (a partnership declined, a public API committed, a naming/positioning lock, a data model that cannot be migrated) is being made in the same pass as routine work, with no note distinguishing it.
- **Premature Automation** — Automating a process before doing it manually encodes a guess about the rules into code, and the code then prevents you from observing the exceptions that would have taught you the real rules. Manual work at small N is not a stopgap, it is the only instrument that reads the requirement. TRIGGER: A spec automates onboarding, setup, matching, curation, routing, or support before that process has been performed by hand enough times to have written down the exception cases.
- **Unlabeled Working Surface** — Surfaces "no user sees" — shell prompts, admin panels, log output, internal dashboards — are used by humans under time pressure, and identical-looking contexts there produce identity confusion at the exact moment stakes are highest. Unseen does not mean unused; it means untested. TRIGGER: Work touches an internal-only surface — CLI output, shell/SSH tooling, admin console, staging vs prod environment, log lines, migration scripts — and the change is justified as "internal, no design needed."
- **Numbers Nobody Measured** — A quantitative claim with no measurement behind it still functions as one downstream — it gets cited, budgeted against, and used to close arguments — because the number's format carries authority its provenance does not. Organizations generate these numbers where the desired answer is known and the measurement is expensive. TRIGGER: A spec, README, or marketing line quotes a reliability, latency, uptime, accuracy, or coverage figure without naming the measurement that produced it.
- **Metric As Taste Proxy** — Delegating small aesthetic judgments to experiments converts every decision into a measurement cost, and because micro-variants have micro-effects, the org optimizes a local hill it can measure while losing the ability to make any decision it cannot. Designers who supply judgment become unable to justify their work and leave. TRIGGER: A design disagreement is proposed to be settled by A/B test, click-through data, or "let's measure it" — particularly on typography, spacing, color, copy tone, or single-pixel values.
- **Premature Scaling** — Capacity built ahead of demand converts fixed costs into a deadline: the burn rate now sets how long you have to find the demand, rather than the demand setting how much you build. Because the capacity is sunk, the team also loses the option to change the shape of the business when the early signal comes back wrong. TRIGGER: A plan that adds headcount, geographies, warehouses, regions, or provisioned infrastructure capacity on a schedule rather than in response to a measured demand signal; "build it so it scales to N" before N/100 exists.

## Set B

- **Compliance As Friction** — A founder who correctly identifies a requirement as wasteful can build a tool that removes the appearance of compliance rather than the requirement, because the tool is faster to build than the argument to win. The evidence of the workaround is durable, discoverable, and personally attributable. TRIGGER: A prompt or diff proposes bypassing, auto-satisfying, or faking a legal, licensing, security-review, audit-log, or approval requirement on the grounds that the requirement is overhead.
- **Pet Cluster Upgrade** — Infrastructure configured by hand and not committed anywhere has no record of what depends on what, so an upgrade that changes an implicit contract (a label, a default, a deprecated field) breaks something nobody can trace back. Recovery becomes archaeology under time pressure. TRIGGER: An in-place upgrade, version bump, or migration of infrastructure described as legacy, hand-built, one-off, or "the original" — or config applied manually rather than from version control.
- **Premature Automation** — Automating a process before doing it manually encodes a guess about the rules into code, and the code then prevents you from observing the exceptions that would have taught you the real rules. Manual work at small N is not a stopgap, it is the only instrument that reads the requirement. TRIGGER: A spec automates onboarding, setup, matching, curation, routing, or support before that process has been performed by hand enough times to have written down the exception cases.
- **Second-System Effect** — The v2 becomes the dumping ground for every idea deferred from v1, and because none of them has shipped, none can be cut on evidence. Scope grows faster than the team can integrate, so the system never reaches a state where any single feature is finishable. TRIGGER: A spec or plan for a v2/next-gen version that lists features deferred from v1, or bundles a new data model + new UI + new extensibility layer in one release.
- **Seat Pricing Punishes Adoption** — When a product's value rises with how many colleagues are on it but the price also rises per colleague, the buyer's rational move is to restrict the rollout — which starves the exact network effect the product depends on. The pricing model actively fights the product's mechanism, and the resulting low penetration reads to the team as weak demand. TRIGGER: A pricing page or spec charging per seat, per user, or per member for a product whose core value requires a whole team, or charging full price for read-only/viewer/observer roles.
- **Refusing The Amputation** — Deferring a feature to 'phase 2' keeps it in the architecture — its data model, its surface area, its coupling — so the v1 carries the full complexity cost while delivering none of the value. Deletion is the only scope cut that actually reduces work; deferral is bookkeeping. TRIGGER: A v1 spec preserves every feature from a prototype or predecessor, and the scope-cut discussion produces deferrals ('phase 2', 'later slice', 'roadmap') rather than removals from the model.
- **Man-Month Arithmetic** — Adding people to a late project adds onboarding cost and communication paths (n²) before it adds throughput, and the work that is late is usually serially dependent, so the added capacity cannot be applied to the critical path. TRIGGER: A plan responding to schedule slip by adding engineers, parallel agents, or extra workstreams to the same late deliverable.
- **One-Way Door Misread** — Anything external parties can build against — a public API, tool name, URL scheme, event type, wire field, file format — becomes unrevocable the moment someone depends on it, because the cost of removal is paid by them and shows up as broken trust rather than a rollback. Such decisions get made at the speed of a reversible one because the diff looks small. TRIGGER: A diff or spec publishes something external parties will depend on: a public API endpoint, an MCP or CLI tool name, an event type or enum value, a URL scheme, a config key, an on-disk or wire format.
- **Reused Component, New Envelope** — A component's correctness depends on the operating envelope it was validated in — value ranges, clock rates, load profile. Reuse carries the code but not the envelope, and the assumption that made it safe becomes silently false. TRIGGER: A diff or plan that lifts an existing module, service, query, or library into a new scale, platform, region, or traffic profile described as reusing it "unchanged" or "as-is".
- **Channel-Price Mismatch** — A product's price point determines which acquisition channels can ever pay back; ad-based channels have a floor CAC set by auction competition, and if that floor exceeds the customer's lifetime contribution the channel is unusable at any spend level. Teams discover this after burning the test budget because they benchmarked click-through, not payback. TRIGGER: A GTM plan or budget line proposing Google/Meta/LinkedIn/Reddit ads, sponsorships, or outbound SDRs without stating ARPU, gross margin, and a payback period in months.
- **Second-System Rewrite** — A rewrite is scoped against the accumulated wishlist of everything the old system got wrong, so the new system is strictly larger than the one that already took years. Meanwhile the old system keeps shipping nothing, and the rewrite's real cost is the undocumented edge cases embedded in the code being discarded. TRIGGER: A proposal to rewrite a working module or surface from scratch, justified by cruft, where the new version is also scoped to fix known limitations of the old one.
- **Positioning To The Wrong Frame** — The category you claim determines which competitors the buyer mentally lines you up against and which features they treat as table stakes. Claim a broad, incumbent-owned category and your genuine differentiator is scored as a minor feature; claim the frame where that differentiator is the whole point and the same product becomes the obvious choice. TRIGGER: Copy or a spec that names a broad established category ("CRM", "project management", "observability", "coordination layer") without naming the specific buyer and the specific alternative they'd otherwise use.
- **The Big-Bang Relaunch** — Replacing a shipped surface wholesale in one release couples a hundred independent bets into a single irreversible event, so you cannot tell which change caused the loss, and existing users pay the full re-learning cost on a day they did not choose. Habitual users are the ones who notice most, and they are the ones you can least afford to lose. TRIGGER: A plan replaces an existing shipped surface in one release — 'v2 replaces v1', 'retire the old frame', 'cut over' — with no dual-run, opt-in, or staged migration.
- **Foundation Before First User** — Infrastructure built for a feature that does not exist has no consumer to falsify its design, so its interface is derived from imagination and calcifies before contact. The dependent feature then inherits constraints from a substrate that was never tested against the real requirement. TRIGGER: A spec adds infrastructure — an index, cache, schema, consolidation pass, worker, centroid set, router — whose only named consumer is a feature not yet built or validated.
- **Silent Mode Divergence** — When one control's meaning depends on a mode the user cannot see at the moment of input, the interface accepts a correct-looking action and executes a different one; the operator's mental model and the machine's state diverge with no feedback to reconcile them. TRIGGER: A diff or spec adds a mode, toggle, view-state, or context in which the same control, keystroke, or input field produces different results — especially when the current mode is shown somewhere other than where the input happens.

## Set C

- **Refusing The Amputation** — Deferring a feature to 'phase 2' keeps it in the architecture — its data model, its surface area, its coupling — so the v1 carries the full complexity cost while delivering none of the value. Deletion is the only scope cut that actually reduces work; deferral is bookkeeping. TRIGGER: A v1 spec preserves every feature from a prototype or predecessor, and the scope-cut discussion produces deferrals ('phase 2', 'later slice', 'roadmap') rather than removals from the model.
- **Product Line Sprawl** — Each additional surface, SKU, or entry point consumes maintenance, documentation, support, and decision bandwidth permanently, while the marginal one is almost always worse than the median — so average quality falls monotonically as count rises, and the team's best people are spread across things no one would start today. TRIGGER: The plan adds a new surface, product, SKU, CLI command, MCP tool, page, or entry point while existing ones are unfinished, unowned, or undocumented; a countable surface inventory grows.
- **Sunk Identity** — A decision defended by prior investment or by "this is what we are" cannot be evaluated, because both premises are facts about the past rather than about the option. The incumbent option gets an unearned advantage precisely in proportion to how much was spent establishing it. TRIGGER: A decision or spec is defended by how much has already been built, how long it took, or by identity language ("we're a X company", "this is our thesis"), rather than by the option's forward value.
- **Discoverability Mistaken For Absence** — Users request features they cannot find, so a request queue reads as a gap list when it is often a findability report; building the requested thing a second time adds surface area without adding capability and makes the original even harder to find. TRIGGER: A ticket or spec proposes building a capability that may already exist somewhere in the product — "add a way to X," "users can't do Y" — without a check of whether X/Y already ships behind a menu, flag, or command.
- **Relearning Tax Ignored** — In a product people use daily, the interface is a motor habit; moving things resets that habit for every existing user at once, so the cost is paid on day one by everyone while the benefit accrues slowly to newcomers who do not exist yet. The new-user gain almost never exceeds the aggregate relearning loss. TRIGGER: A spec reorganizes navigation, moves an existing feature to a new location, renames a primary action, or changes the information architecture of a product that already has habituated daily users.
- **Exit Reason Assumed** — Churn explanations default to the most legible variable — price, salary, performance — because it is the one already instrumented. The actual reasons are relational and only obtainable by asking, so the retention fix targets the wrong lever and produces no change, which is then read as the problem being intractable. TRIGGER: An explanation of attrition, churn, drop-off, or non-adoption asserts a cause (price, speed, missing feature) without citing anything the departed users or teammates actually said.
- **Sustaining-Only Roadmap** — Every roadmap item that improves the product for your best existing customers is individually rational and collectively fatal, because the customers who fund you today also define the axis on which you cannot be disrupted — and the cheaper, worse alternative improves along a different axis until it is good enough. The signal to move never arrives from your own revenue. TRIGGER: Every roadmap item deepens the product for existing or highest-paying users, and a cheaper, simpler, or lower-fidelity alternative in the market is dismissed as a toy or 'not our customer'.
- **The Metric That Moves Alone** — A proxy metric that can rise without the underlying job being served will be optimized directly, because it is the thing that is measured and reported; the product then drifts toward whatever produces the number, and the gap between metric and job widens fastest exactly when the metric looks best. TRIGGER: A success criterion is a count that can increase while the underlying job goes unserved — events emitted, broadcasts sent, tool calls, DAU, sign-ups, page views — with no paired measure of whether the recipient acted on it.
- **Chesterton's Dead Code** — Code that appears unused is often reachable through a path nobody has in mind — an old flag, a stale server, a legacy client — so removing the guard while leaving the code, or reusing its switch for a new meaning, arms a landmine that only fires under partial deployment. TRIGGER: A diff that deletes or repurposes a feature flag, retry, guard clause, or branch labeled unused/deprecated/legacy without explaining what originally set it.
- **Job Mistaken For Demographic** — Segmenting by who the user is produces feature requests that correlate with the segment but explain nothing causal, so improvements aimed at the persona miss the situation that actually triggered the purchase. Two people in the same demographic hire the product for opposite jobs, and averaging them produces a product for neither. TRIGGER: Requirements are segmented by who the user is (role, persona, seniority, company size, 'founders vs engineers') rather than by the situation and task at the moment of use.
- **Stated Preference Trap** — Users accurately report what they want and cannot report what it costs, because the cost (latency, density, decision load) is a property of the system, not of the wish. Delivering the requested thing literally can move the metric the wrong way. TRIGGER: A spec justifies a feature with "users asked for it," a feature-request count, survey results, or a support-ticket theme — with no statement of what the addition costs in load time, screen density, or steps.
- **Segment-Blind PMF Survey** — The Sean Ellis 40% test averages across everyone who signed up, and a product with intense fit for a narrow segment plus indifference from tourists scores the same as a product nobody loves. The average hides the segment that would tell you what to build, so teams conclude they have no fit and pivot away from the users who actually had it. TRIGGER: A proposal to run the 40% test, NPS, or a satisfaction survey across the full user list, or a stated PMF/NPS number reported as one figure with no segment breakdown.
- **No Beachhead** — A message aimed at everyone is specific to no one, so it fails the only test that matters early: can one user explain it to the next user in a sentence. Without a defined first customer, price, channel, and objection-handling all become guesses, and there is no dense population in which word of mouth can propagate. TRIGGER: TAM-driven sizing, "for any team / any company / everyone who...", or a launch plan listing three or more personas with no ranking of which one ships first.
- **Parity Roadmap** — Building a feature because a competitor has it imports their strategy and their constraints without their distribution, and a checklist match gives an existing user of the incumbent no reason to switch — matching is by definition not a differentiator. It also consumes the scarce build capacity that would have produced one. TRIGGER: A requirement is justified by a competitor having it — 'X ships this', 'table stakes', 'we need parity', a feature-comparison matrix, or a decision that would change if that competitor disappeared.
- **Boiling The Ocean** — When v1 requires three or more unproven components to all work at once, the probability of shipping is the product of each part's odds, and no single part can be validated until the others exist. Learning is deferred until the whole thing is done, which is exactly when it is most expensive to be wrong. TRIGGER: A spec or plan lists three or more novel, unproven components (new runtime, new data model, new rendering surface, new protocol) that must all work before anything is usable.
